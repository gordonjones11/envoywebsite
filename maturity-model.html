The Intelligent Factory:   The Maturity Model

Where is your mill on the curve?

Unstable, Stable, Repeatable, Optimized, Adaptive. Every mill sits somewhere on this five-level curve. Knowing where you are matters more than knowing where you're headed, because it tells you what will work next and what will fail.

The Model

Five levels of operational maturity.

Maturity is not a measure of how much technology a mill owns. It is a measure of how decisions get made, and these are the words an operation already uses about itself. Each level marks a real shift in how the mill detects, decides, and learns, and each has a trap that keeps mills stuck there.

1

Unstable

Firefighting. Problems find you. Knowledge lives in a few experienced heads, shifts firefight independently, and yesterday's issue repeats next month. The trap: heroic firefighting becomes normal, and the people best at it get rewarded for it.

2

Stable

You know what is wrong and you are fixing it. Problems are surfaced deliberately rather than discovered late, findings get owned, and the list gets shorter instead of longer. The trap: mistaking finding more problems for improving performance. A signal nobody owns is noise.

3

Repeatable

Everyone runs it the same way. The best-known way to run each grade at each rate is written down, and crews run to it instead of to personal preference. Shift-to-shift variation drops because there is something objective to return to. The trap: a standard that exists on paper and nobody actually runs to.

4

Optimized

You know the best way, not just a consistent way. The standard has been tested against cost rather than only against consistency, so the targets reflect where total cost is lowest for this grade, this rate, these constraints. The trap: optimizing one area at the expense of the whole mill.

5

Adaptive

What you learn gets baked back in. Every upset, every recovery, every investigation updates the centerlines, the thresholds, the runbooks and the procedures, so the mill keeps what it learns instead of one person keeping it. The trap: confusing adaptation with automation. The goal is a mill that learns faster, not one that runs itself.

Why This Matters

Most industrial AI fails because it is sold at the wrong maturity stage.

You cannot centerline a process that is broken. You cannot optimize a process that is not repeatable. Vendors sell optimization into unstable mills, it fails, and the organization concludes that AI does not work in manufacturing. The conclusion is wrong. The sequencing was.

Stable before repeatable

A process that breaks unpredictably cannot be standardized, because there is no stable behavior to standardize around. Fix first.

Repeatable before optimized

If three crews run the same grade three different ways, there is no operating point to optimize. There are three, and you cannot tell which result came from which.

Optimized before adaptive

Learning compounds only when there is a standard for the learning to update. Without one, every lesson is absorbed by an individual and lost at the next retirement.

Adaptive is not simply last

It is the capability that makes the first four compound instead of decay. A mill that stops learning slides back down the curve.

Moving Up

What actually has to change.

Each step up the curve is a change in how the mill works, not a change in what it owns.

↑

Unstable to Stable

Someone reviews the operation every day, and the findings get owned and closed. Not a dashboard somebody could look at. A review that happens, with a name attached to each finding.

↑

Stable to Repeatable

The best-known way to run is written down by grade and by rate, and crews run to it. This is the step most mills skip, and it is why their optimization work never holds.

↑

Repeatable to Optimized

The standard gets tested against cost, not just against consistency. Consistent and expensive is still expensive.

↑

Optimized to Adaptive

What each event teaches is written back into the standard, not into a folder. The test is simple: when the same problem returns in six months, does the second investigation cost less than the first?

Where Are You?

A mill is rarely at one level.

Most operations are further along on quality than on reliability, or the other way round. That is why the assessment scores each of the five decisions against these five levels rather than giving the mill a single grade. Five decisions, five levels, one grid, and the output is not a number but a sentence: repeatable on quality, unstable on reliability, so start there.
